Project 01 · Digital banking

AIB Bank: Redesigning Pay & Transfer

AIB is one of Ireland's largest banks, and Pay & Transfer is one of the most used features in the app. My work was to audit that flow against the bar challenger banks had quietly reset across Europe, then design and build a working proposal — screen by screen — that felt just as fast, without losing what a regulated bank has to get right.

RoleProduct Designer
CategoryRetail banking, Ireland
ScopeUX audit, benchmarking, interaction design
PlatformApp (iOS & Android)

Section

Context

A core banking flow, held to a challenger-bank bar.

Pay & Transfer is where a banking app either earns trust or loses patience: it's where people move their own money between accounts, pay someone new, pay a bill, or top up a phone. AIB's version worked, but it was designed in an era before Revolut, N26 and Monzo reset what “fast” means for a transfer.

Section

Journey audit

Three moments where the flow quietly lost people.

I mapped the existing journey end to end, from choosing a payee to confirming a transfer, looking for the exact steps where hesitation or drop-off was most likely.

01
Adding a new payee was intimidating

IBAN format guidance was unclear, with no real-time validation, so people only found out they'd made a mistake after submitting.

02
The flow gave almost no feedback

Fields filled in silently, with no confirmation that a step had gone well — a poor fit for a task that involves someone's money.

03
The daily limit felt like a wall, not a guardrail

At €10,000 a day, the limit is a reasonable security measure, but the app offered no clear, self-service way to raise it when a real need came up.

Section

Design principle

Make the safe path also the fast path.

The goal wasn't just to shorten the flow. It was to make the shortest path also the clearest one — because when a transfer involves someone's money, speed and safety aren't opposites to trade off. They're the same design problem seen from two angles.

Section

Decisions

Each of these started as a constraint the audit surfaced. I designed, then built, the screens that resolve it — not mockups of the idea, the actual interaction.

Decision 01

Real-time IBAN validation, with the bank confirmed automatically

The existing form showed a static IBAN example with no real-time check. People only discovered a typo after submitting. I proposed validating as the person types, auto-detecting the receiving bank the instant the IBAN is well-formed — and an invalid IBAN gets a specific message about which group is short, not just “invalid”.

Decision 02

Pay a Contact becomes the default path, not a hidden setting

AIB already had the building blocks for this: a “Pay a Contact” option existed, backed by SEPA Instant, but it lived behind an extra opt-in setup step, tucked in a separate tab, disconnected from the main flow most people used. I proposed making it the first, highlighted option on the main menu instead of an easter egg.

Paying a phone contact instantly isn't a new idea globally — it's the same underlying pattern behind Brazil's Pix. Even where AIB had already started building it, it hadn't yet earned a place in the primary journey.

Decision 03

Visual feedback at every step that touches money

Fields filled in silently before, with nothing confirming a step had gone well. I designed a purpose-built confirmation for each moment: a green check on a valid field, a live SEPA Instant toggle, and a receipt with share and save actions once a payment lands.

Decision 04

A self-service path to a higher limit, not a hard wall

Rather than treating €10,000 as a fixed wall, I proposed a self-service option to request a temporary increase — picking an amount, re-confirming with Face ID, done in under a minute, and reverting automatically after 24 hours.

Section

The full flow

Fourteen states, one working prototype.

Beyond the four decisions above, the full flow was designed and built end to end — from choosing how to add a payee, through scanning an IBAN, to confirming with Face ID. Rebuilt inside AIB's real visual system: dark theme, teal identity, the same grid and section conventions already live in the app.

Section

Competitive benchmarking

Reading the market before redesigning a single screen.

Before proposing anything, I ran a structured benchmark of the digital banks that had already reset expectations in this space — not to copy them, but to understand which patterns had already become the baseline a traditional bank would be silently compared against.

01
Revolut

Simple, intuitive payee addition, real-time IBAN and payee validation, instant payments to phone contacts. Applied as the model for real-time validation and instant payments.

02
N26

Flexible payment limits with a temporary increase option, and clear visual feedback throughout the transfer. Applied as the model for the limit-increase flow and step-by-step feedback.

03
Monzo

A guided, step-by-step assistant for adding payees, with contact integration and instant payment services built in. Applied as the model for the payee assistant and contacts integration.

Zooming out past Europe, Brazil's Pix was a useful reference point too: a phone number resolving instantly to a real transfer, no IBAN required. Different regulatory context, but the UX lesson travels well — resolving an identity to a payee should feel closer to sending a text than filling out a form.

Success criteria and impact

Defining “better” before building it.

Part of proposing a redesign to a regulated bank is being explicit about how success would be measured, not just what would change on screen.

  • Transfer completion rate: fewer people abandoning mid-flow
  • Average transfer time: less time from intent to confirmation
  • Support cost: fewer errors from invalid IBANs and unclear limits
  • User satisfaction: tracked through direct feedback and surveys

Beyond this specific flow, my broader work at AIB during this period included well-defined prototyping processes that reduced development time, a 15% increase in engagement measured through user research and A/B testing, and WCAG 2.1 accessibility improvements that made the app usable for more people, not just faster for the average one.

What this project reinforced is that in banking, speed and safety aren't opposites to trade off — they're the same design problem seen from two angles. The fastest flow is the one where the person never has to wonder if it worked.

Note: this case reflects a UX audit and redesign proposal developed during my time at AIB Bank. Some details were adapted to preserve confidential information. Prototype screens were rebuilt independently to match AIB's public visual identity.
